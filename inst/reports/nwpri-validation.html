Simulating with uncertainty: agreement with NONMEM $PRIOR NWPRI

Validation of run_sim(n_uncertainty = ) in pharmr.extra · NONMEM 7.6.0 · 2026-08-20

Verdict. The two agree. Over 1000 parameter draws from the same fit, means and SDs of the fixed effects match to within 0.3% and 2.4%, those of the variance parameters to within 4.4% and 7.3%, and the resulting 90% uncertainty interval on the predicted concentration profile to within 6.3%. The remaining differences are structural (different prior families, different block independence assumptions), not defects — and on the one that matters, our draws lose less information than NWPRI's.

What was compared

run_sim(n_uncertainty = N) draws N parameter sets from the fit's covariance matrix and runs one simulation per draw. NONMEM does the same thing with $PRIOR NWPRI + $SIMULATION ... TRUE=PRIOR. Bit-for-bit equality is impossible — the two use different RNG streams and different sampling distributions for the variance parameters — so the comparison is on aggregates.

Both sides drew 1000 parameter sets from one reference fit: a 1-compartment oral model (ADVAN2 TRANS2), 60 subjects, 10 samples each, IIV on CL and V, proportional residual error, fitted with FOCE INTER and a full $COVARIANCE step. NWPRI priors were centred on that fit: a multivariate normal on the THETA block using the estimated THETA covariance, and inverse-Wishart priors on OMEGA and SIGMA with df = 2 · (estimate / SE)2 + 1 (Gisleskog, Karlsson & Beal 2002 — the same rule PsN applies).

Where the two differ, and why

1. Prior family for the variance parameters

NWPRI draws OMEGA and SIGMA from inverse-Wishart distributions, which are right-skewed (skew 0.26–0.78 in the table above). Pharmpy draws every parameter from a single truncated multivariate normal, which is symmetric (skew ≈ 0). The first two moments still line up to within a few percent, so summary statistics and percentile bands are close; the far right tail of a variance parameter is slightly heavier under NWPRI.

2. Independence between prior blocks

NWPRI treats the THETA prior, each OMEGA block and the SIGMA prior as mutually independent. It therefore discards the THETA–OMEGA and THETA–SIGMA covariances that the $COVARIANCE step reports. Our sampler draws all parameters jointly from the reported covariance matrix and keeps them:

Both reproduce the within-block correlation. Only ours reproduces the cross-block one. This is a case where the difference favours our implementation — NWPRI's block independence is a modelling convenience of the prior formulation, not a property of the estimated uncertainty.

Why this approach rather than delegating to NWPRI

We could have implemented uncertainty simulation by rewriting the control stream to add $PRIOR NWPRI and handing the job to NONMEM. We sample in R/Pharmpy instead, for three reasons.

  • Not NONMEM-specific. The sampler consumes a point-estimate vector and a covariance matrix — nothing else. The same code path serves nlmixr2 fits, and any future backend, unchanged. $PRIOR NWPRI exists only in NONMEM, so delegating to it would have left every non-NONMEM model without uncertainty propagation.
  • No modification of the model file. NWPRI requires injecting $THETAP, $THETAPV, $OMEGAP, $OMEGAPD, $SIGMAP and $SIGMAPD records into the control stream, generated from the fit and kept consistent with the $OMEGA/$SIGMA block structure. That is fragile (block-diagonal OMEGA, fixed elements, BLOCK SAME) and it means the model that gets simulated is no longer the model that was fitted. Our approach updates initial estimates per draw and leaves model structure untouched.
  • Uses the full reported uncertainty. As shown above, the prior-block formulation throws away cross-block covariances. Sampling directly from the covariance matrix does not.

The known cost is the symmetric-normal draw for the variance parameters (difference 1), examined next.

Is inverse-Wishart the more appropriate choice, statistically?

Difference 1 is the one worth arguing about, so it is worth stating why we are comfortable with the normal draw. Short answer: no, inverse-Wishart is not the better-justified choice here — though it is not wrong either, and it is better on one specific axis.

The normal draw is the one with asymptotic backing

What is being propagated is estimation uncertainty: the sampling distribution of the estimator Ω̂. Asymptotically that distribution is normal, for variance components exactly as much as for fixed effects — √n(θ̂ − θ) → N(0, I−1). The matrix NONMEM reports from $COVARIANCE is that asymptotic approximation. Drawing from a multivariate normal defined by it uses the object for exactly what it is.

Inverse-Wishart is not the sampling distribution of Ω̂. It is the conjugate prior for a covariance matrix in the multivariate normal model. That is why NWPRI uses it: $PRIOR exists to specify priors for a subsequent penalised or Bayesian estimation, where conjugacy and guaranteed positive-definiteness are the properties you want. TRUE=PRIOR simulation is a secondary use of that same machinery, and it inherits the inverse-Wishart form — it was not selected because it describes estimator uncertainty.

A detail from the run above is consistent with this. NWPRI's variance draws centre 2–4% above the point estimate; ours centre on it. That is what you would expect if the estimate is anchored as the mode of the inverse-Wishart rather than its mean — an artifact of the prior specification, carrying no information about Ω̂.

Where inverse-Wishart is genuinely better

  • Support. Inverse-Wishart draws are positive-definite by construction. Our normal draws are rejected and resampled when they fall outside the parameter bounds or yield a non-positive-definite OMEGA block. The retained draws are then a truncated normal: mean biased upward, variance shrunk, shape not cleanly characterised.
  • Skew is real. Variance estimators are right-skewed in finite samples (compare σ̂2 ∼ scaled χ2). Inverse-Wishart reproduces that; a normal approximation cannot.

Both only bite when a variance parameter is imprecisely estimated. For a diagonal element the rejection rate is P(draw < 0) = Φ(−1/RSE):

RSE of variance parameter20%30%40%50%70%
draws rejected<0.01%0.04%0.6%2.3%7.6%

In the anchor fit RSE is 16–18%, which is why the measured skew of our draws came out at ≈ 0 and the two samplers agreed to a few percent. The distinction only becomes material well outside that range.

Where inverse-Wishart is worse

  • One degree-of-freedom parameter per block. A BLOCK(n) OMEGA has n(n+1)/2 elements whose estimator uncertainty is itself a full matrix. Inverse-Wishart compresses all of it into a single scalar ν plus the scale matrix, so it cannot express differing precision across elements and it ties variance and correlation uncertainty together. This is the standard objection to inverse-Wishart in the Bayesian literature (Alvarez, Niemi & Simpson 2014; Tokuda et al.).
  • No cross-block covariance — difference 2 above.
  • Crude moment match. The df = 2 · (estimate / SE)2 + 1 rule matches the marginal variance of one diagonal element. Applied to a block it is an approximation.

What would actually be better than either

If the goal is to fix the truncation weakness, inverse-Wishart is not the move — sampling on a transformed scale is. Draw log(ω2) for diagonal elements, or a matrix-log / Cholesky parameterisation for blocks, with the covariance matrix carried across by the delta method. That gets all four properties at once:

  • the support constraint holds by construction, so no rejection and no truncation bias;
  • right skew on the natural scale — the thing inverse-Wishart gets right;
  • the full joint covariance is retained, including THETA–OMEGA — the thing inverse-Wishart gets wrong;
  • no per-block degree-of-freedom bottleneck.

And when the normal approximation itself is the problem, rather than merely its support, the answer is to drop the approximation: SIR (already in this package — add_sir.R, vignettes/sir-covariance.Rmd) reweights normal proposals by the actual likelihood, and a bootstrap avoids the distributional assumption entirely.

Conclusion. Switching to inverse-Wishart would trade an asymptotically justified approximation for a conjugate-prior convenience, and would cost cross-block covariance and per-element precision. For well-estimated models the two are indistinguishable in practice, as measured above. The genuine gap is imprecisely-estimated variance parameters, and log / Cholesky-scale sampling closes that gap better than inverse-Wishart would.

Reproducing this

The comparison is an anchored regression test. The NONMEM side is generated once and frozen as a fixture; the Pharmpy side is re-sampled at test time and checked against it, so the test needs Pharmpy but not NONMEM and runs in CI.

  • tests/testthat/test-run_sim-nwpri.R — 6 tests, 36 assertions. Asserts the agreement above and both structural differences explicitly, so a change in either sampler surfaces instead of hiding inside a loose tolerance.
  • tests/testthat/fixtures/nwpri_anchor.rds — model code, point estimates, SEs, covariance matrix, and the 1000 NWPRI draws.
  • tests/testthat/fixtures/_create-nwpri-anchor.R — regenerates the fixture end to end (simulate data → fit → build NWPRI prior → SUBPROBLEMS=1000 TRUE=PRIOR). Fully seeded. Needs NONMEM, so it is run by hand in the pmx container:
docker run --rm \
  -v "$PWD/tests/testthat/fixtures:/fixtures" \
  -v "$PWD:/pkg" \
  -e RETICULATE_PYTHON=/app/venv/bin/python \
  -w /fixtures --entrypoint sh pmx:latest \
  -c 'Rscript _create-nwpri-anchor.R'

Two gotchas worth recording

  • NWPRI needs an explicit PLEV= under TRUE=PRIOR. Without it NONMEM stops with VALUE OF ARGUMENT 'PLEV' IS INAPPROPRIATE. The fixture uses PLEV=0.9999 so THETA truncation is negligible and the draws stay comparable to an untruncated multivariate normal.
  • Pharmpy 1.8.0 in the pmx image cannot read a NONMEM fit under NumPy 2 — read_modelfit_results() calls np.fill_diagonal(cor.values, 1) and fails with ValueError: underlying array is read-only. Upstream bug, unrelated to this package; the fixture script reads .ext and .cov directly and sidesteps it.
